# CLAUDE.md — Rumah Adat Nusantara

Working instructions for Claude Code. Read PRD.md for what is being built and DESIGN.md for how it must look. This file is how to work on it.

## Current state

Thirty-five entries on a tradition-neutral core, all thirty-five on screen — twenty-six houses, two granaries, three halls, a palace, a boat, a stone tomb, a cremation tower, a cluster and a pair of ancestors.** The last twelve are the ones that show how far the neutral contract stretches: it never asked whether a building stands still, whether anybody lives in it, whether it will exist tomorrow, whether part of it is alive, whether it narrows as it rises, how many buildings a `Built` contains, whether they are all the same *kind* of thing, or whether the thing that makes a building worth including can be checked at all.

- **The project is called Rumah Adat Nusantara.** It was called Tongkonan while there was one house, and Pasak — the peg that pins a mortise and tenon — while the point was two houses meeting one core. With four houses and more intended, the collection is the subject and the name says what a visitor finds. It is a proper name, identical in both locales. The repository is `rumah-adat-nusantara` and the site serves from that path — the rename is complete. Git remotes at the old repository name redirect; the old Pages URL does not, which is why the rename waited until the author asked for it.
- **The collection has a front door and so does each house.** `/[locale]/` is the landing: the claim, the story of how one house became several, the sites plotted on a real coastline (Natural Earth's `ne_10m_land`, public domain, clipped and simplified into `lib/geo/nusantara.ts` by `scripts/coastline.mjs` and vendored — never fetched; the plotted coordinates are the same ones the solar arithmetic runs on, and `test/geo.test.ts` asserts every house stands on land), and an index card per house with its own provenance bar. `/[locale]/[tradisi]/` is that house's front door: whose house, the caution, the orientation constraint, its split, and the four routes as described doors. The landing and the index read everything from the registry and their copy carries no counts, so a fifth house appears on them without either page changing.
- **The landing draws the houses it claims.** `lib/core/silhouette.ts` projects a built house's parts onto a vertical plane (the one its ridge runs along, from `SceneModel.ridgeAxis`), rasterises them into an occupancy grid, traces the filled/empty boundary into closed loops and simplifies them — pure arithmetic, tradition-neutral, tested over every registry entry in `test/silhouette.test.ts`. `components/Elevation.tsx` renders the loops: the landing's hero is every house at one scale on as many ground lines as it takes — `lib/draw/shelf.ts` packs them into rows in registry order and every row is drawn in the same viewBox width, so metres per pixel is a constant of the sheet rather than a property of a row, with a scale bar whose width is a fraction of that same viewBox (the same-scale claim cannot silently break, and a fifteenth house is packed by the same arithmetic and adds a row if it needs one); the index cards carry minis in a shared frame for the same reason; each front door carries its own elevation sheet plus the generator's readouts. Nothing is illustrated — a silhouette that disagrees with the model is impossible because it is computed from the model's parts. The landing widened to `max-w-5xl` for the shelf; prose stays at its measure. The same loops make every other picture of the site: the first house's silhouette (`public/tongkonan.svg`, held against the generator by `test/icon.test.ts`) and the share cards (`public/og/<locale>/` — the shelf for the collection, one card per house per locale, drawn by `test/og.gen.ts`'s hand-written rasteriser and PNG encoder, no font file and no new dependency, and held against the generator by pixel comparison in `test/og.test.ts`, which also fails when a thirty-sixth house has no card; regenerate with `WRITE_OG=1` / `WRITE_ICON=1`). The exported 404 is a real bilingual page (`app/(splash)/tidak-ditemukan/`) copied over `404.html` by the build script, because a static export answers a wrong address with one file from the host.

The four old tradition-less paths still answer and say where the page went, carrying the query with them, because a citable address that stops resolving is not much of a citation.
- **The index knows its geography, and the collection can be read two at a time.** Every `Site` names its island group (`Island` in `lib/solar/position.ts` — a fact about the site, beside its coordinates), and the landing's index arranges itself by island west to east, both computed, so a thirty-sixth house files itself; the plate number stays as a stamp of collection history and a gloss says so. A type-to-filter (`components/IndexFilter.tsx`, an attribute contract with the server-rendered cards, mounted only after hydration) narrows by name, people, place, or island. The shelf's labels are annotations, not links — the index below is the one enumeration. On touch, the map names before it opens (`components/MapDoors.tsx`: first tap opens the plaque, second navigates; hit boxes padded past the glyphs), and a keyboard reader can skip the map in one stop. `/[locale]/banding/` is the comparison: two buildings on one ground line at one scale via the same shelf, each with its own figures and its own provenance bar, nothing merged — adjacency, never the morph; the pair rides `?a=&b=` on one exported page per locale, built client-side like the reading route. The canvas's accessible name is a template the route fills (`test/copy.test.ts` holds the placeholder — it said "tongkonan" over every house, the one neutrality leak the architecture tests cannot see). On `/bangun` an arriving address with rules missing is completed to the canonical query and the filling-in is admitted in the rail; a reset back to the default rules appears once there is somewhere to come back from. The shelf's entrance staggers by row, not by house, so the whole drawing stands within a breath of the layout timing. `/sumber` puts the dimensions and the bibliography before the sensitivity and counterexample rooms, because the stated first reader is a student.

- **A caption stands on the ground it names, and the ground is drawn where it was meant to be.** Chasing one label that sat on a wall turned up three faults under it. `captionAt` in `lib/core/scene.ts` places a figure's caption: the mean of its vertices when the figure stands beside the house, and the middle of the widest gap between eave and figure edge when it surrounds one — sixteen of the thirty-five were anchored at their own centre, which is where the house is, so they labelled the roof. It lives in the core because it is arithmetic on a plan, and `test/caption.test.ts` holds every figure clear of the drip envelope and inside its own bounds. Under that: **eight traditions passed a width and a depth to `groundRect`, which wants a far corner** — the rumah kebaya's plot was drawn 24 × 33 m under a caption reading 16 × 22, and its road covered the house; the bade's crossroads was two offset bars; the benteng was a rectangle where its own volumes said a wall corner. `groundBox(cx, cz, w, h)` now says it the way a `SiteVolume` does, which is the vocabulary the scene files think in, and the ten figures are drawn from the numbers their glosses already printed. And the rumah kebaya's mark, which held a boundary, a road and two neighbours under one label, is two: a caption can only stand in one place, so a figure that is three places is two figures.
- **There is no tradition tab row.** The rail carries a breadcrumb — which house this is, linking to its front door, and "Semua rumah" up to the landing. Switching houses goes up through the index and back down: the switch is a cut between rule packs, and the query half of the address does not survive it. The tab strip stopped scaling at four and a list page scales indefinitely.
- **The interface has a night register; the building keeps its sun.** A `prefers-color-scheme: dark` block in `globals.css` swaps soot and lime's roles — soot the ground, kapur the ink, rara lifted in value the way riri drops by day, every dark pair's ratio stated beside its token. There is no toggle: the site keeps no state anywhere and a theme control would be its first. `--on-riri` exists because riri is the one pigment that never changes value, so ink over it may not follow `--bolu`. The renderer reads none of these tokens — its constants are its own and its light is solar — so the model stays a daylight object and the dark chrome meets the viewport like a mat around a print. See "The night register" in DESIGN.md.

- **A share card is a sheet, and it is lettered.** The cards carried no text
  on the reasoning that a link's title and description are already text beside
  the image. That is true of a page and false of a chat, where the thumbnail
  arrives first and small — and thirty-five wordless silhouettes at thumbnail
  size are thirty-five dark shapes. So a card now has a title block: the
  collection at the head, the plate number opposite, the house on its ground
  line, and its name and place beneath, each line coming down in size rather
  than running off the sheet. **`test/lettering.ts` is a stroke alphabet** —
  each glyph a set of polylines on a grid (baseline 0, cap 14, x-height 10,
  descender −4), walked with a round pen of one weight. There is still no font
  file: the vendored Plex is woff2, which would mean brotli plus the woff2
  glyf reconstruction to read. It is not a workaround — it is what lettering
  on a drawing is, and a test asserts the alphabet can letter every name and
  place in the registry, so a house named with a character nobody drew fails
  before it ships rather than at generation time. **The cards are per locale**
  (`public/og/<locale>/`), because a name and a place are written in the
  reader's language and a picture is the one part of a shared link that cannot
  fall back. The collection's card asks `lib/draw/shelf.ts` a different
  question from the landing's: the row target is swept and the packing with
  the most pixels per metre wins, a tie broken on evenness so first-fit stops
  leaving one house alone on the last ground line. `Placed` carries its own
  baseline, because a shelf that wraps has more than one ground line.
- **The site is signed.** `components/MakerSignature.tsx` is a quiet credit —
  the author's name linking to their portfolio, the build year set in the
  measuring face, and four 18px stroke marks — at the foot of every page
  shell: the landing, a house's front door, the rail on the four routes, the
  comparison page, the moved pages, the splash and the 404. It shares whatever
  seam is already there rather than adding one; on the front door and the
  comparison page it sits opposite the thing already on that seam, and on a
  phone it stacks under it. **It is kept apart from anything the site owes
  somebody else** — a licence, a data credit and a source table are
  obligations, and this is a signature; running them together would make the
  signature read as a legal notice. The identity is one array at the top of
  the file. The phrase is localised like every other string, because a site
  whose default locale is Indonesian should not say one English sentence at
  the bottom of every page; the name and the year are not.
- **The year is baked in `next.config.mjs`, not read in the component.**
  `Sheet` is a server component but the four route clients import it, so it
  reaches the client bundle — a `new Date()` inside `MakerSignature` would run
  in the browser and disagree with the committed HTML the moment the build
  year went stale, hydrate-mismatching on every page. `env` inlines one value
  into both bundles, so the two cannot disagree.
- **A walk round all thirty-five found three faults the suite had passed, and one of them was the cameras.** Every house was rendered from every preset view and looked at. *The views:* "Dari halaman" put the eye at each tradition's `approachAt` without asking whether that point was outside the building — it stood inside the mbaru niang's thatch, under the rumah gadang's eave and against the face of a tongkonan — and "Tampak muka" was a fixed two-thirds of the three-quarter distance, which cropped every roof taller than its house is wide. The yard view now keeps the tradition's bearing and steps back along it until the eye is clear of the plan and the whole front fits; the elevation is fitted to the front's real extents; the under-floor view of a house with no under-floor (honai, ume kbubu) crouches outside instead of dropping beneath the ground. And the setting was hiding houses square-on — a neighbour's roof across the road from the rumah kebaya, a row of sa'o in front of the ngadhu and bhaga — against the condition it was accepted under, so a site solid that stands between the eye and the house is moved to a layer the view does not draw (the sun's camera still does, so its shadow stays; water is never set aside). *The honai:* thirty-two posts of thirteen centimetres round a twelve-metre circumference left the wall two-thirds air, and `checkNoWindow` passed it by counting posts. A board lining, cut by the doorway, closes it; the check now walks the lining's own vertices and refuses any gap that is not the door, and a test cuts a window to prove it. *The ume kbubu:* the thatch courses were cut edge to edge, so every join was an open slot round the dome, and nothing closed the circumference under the eave on either wall form — the low timber wall was a dimension in the table and a metre of open air in the model. The courses lap through `courseBands` like every other thatched roof here, the foot is built of the form's material with the door cut in it, and `checkNoOtherOpening` checks both, with tests that strip each one away. Both packs gained a declared dimension and their interpolated counts went up, the honest direction.

Nothing about this house is better sourced than the others. Do not read the number as progress, and do not let a future pack chase it by declaring fewer dimensions.

  - **Everything is flat and everything is a footprint or an edge.** No plants, no terrain, no water, no sky, and no modelled barn: a plausible landscape would state, fluently and without provenance, far more than anybody measured, and a guessed rangkiang would turn an honestly named absence into an invented building. Nothing in the setting can hide any part of a house, which is the condition the whole idea was accepted under.
  - **The distances are ordinary dimensions and the bar moved.** Every site figure's metres are declared in its pack, so every house's interpolated share went up — the fourth time in this project that the honest change made the number worse. `SiteMark.provenance` is about the *arrangement*: canon where a source states that the barns stand opposite, interpolated where the author placed the fence. The legend on `/baca` prints which.
- **Interface tokens live in `app/globals.css` and nowhere else.** Six type steps, a 4px spacing scale, and a palette that states its contrast ratio beside every pair the interface uses. `tailwind.config.ts` maps utility names onto them and declares no values of its own. A bracketed size or colour in a component (`text-[13px]`, `bg-[rgba(...)]`) is a bug — it escapes the scale the same way a hardcoded dimension escapes provenance.

**The thirty-fifth building is the Sumbawa Dalam Loka, and its plan is a number out of a text.** The sultanate's palace at Sumbawa Besar, raised on ninety-nine posts for the ninety-nine names of God. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 20 interpolated (83%).

- **Every other tally here counts something present** — households in a betang, clans in a baileo, hearths in a khaim, seats in a sasadu, shoulders under a bade, sleeping bodies in a sudung — and every one could come out differently next year. This one is given, exact, and not the builders' to change.
- **A count that fixes a shape.** Ninety-nine is nine elevens, so the frame is nine post lines one way and eleven the other and the only choice left is which way round. No other rule in the collection reaches from a number to a plan.
- **`checkNinetyNine` has two halves and the second is the one that matters.** Any building can be given ninety-nine posts by standing eleven ornamental ones in its corners; what makes the count a fact about a frame is that every one of them is under a beam, which the check walks the parts to establish.
- **The limit is a text's.** Posts cannot be added, so a larger palace is a wider grid — and the counterexample stretches it until a single beam will not cross from one line to the next, where the only fix is a post mid-span, which is exactly what the rule forbids. The Baduy pole and the woloan lorry are limits of transport, the Korowai trunk and the Rimba carry are limits of what the world provides, the Betawi setback is a neighbour's. This one is a doctrine's.
- **The one figure a tape measure can check and cannot correct:** a survey returning ninety-seven posts would be a finding about a restoration, not about the rule.

**The thirty-fourth building is a Riau balai selaso jatuh kembar, and its floor steps down.** A middle floor where the council sits, and along each side an aisle whose floor has fallen one step below it. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 25 interpolated (86%).

- **The first stepped floor here that steps down.** The limas seats a guest on the step matching their standing, the gadang raises an anjuang for its laras, the malige lifts a storey for a sultan — all of them raise, and all of them say something about a person. This lowers, and says something about an activity: **passing through is not being present.** `checkPassWithoutEntering` is the measurable half — both aisles run clear end to end.
- **`checkOneStepNotAStair` has its two numbers in different hands.** The fall is what the custom wants legible; how big a step a body crosses without noticing is not the custom's to decide. The counterexample deepens the fall — which states the distinction more firmly — until the aisles stop being usable as the way through.
- **The anjung raises a floor and is checked to stay outside the room**, because a raised floor *inside* would be the Palembang claim, which this building does not make.
- Three of the recurring faults again: aisle bearers carried by nothing, a wedge written between two floors that never touch, and platforms beyond the frame standing on air.

**The thirty-third building is a Sahu sasadu, and it states a difference in headroom.** A village hall in west Halmahera, open on all four sides, with several entrances cut to different heights. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 25 interpolated (86%).

- **The only pack here that says standing in centimetres over somebody's head.** The other thirty-two use size, position, floor height, counts, tiers, brackets or stacked boards. `checkDoorsAreNotAlike` is the only check in the project whose subject is that several things of one kind must *differ*, in a stated order — **the exact inverse of the Maluku baileo's `checkPlacesAreEqual`**, and both are true about their own village.
- **`checkEverybodyBows` keeps that from being humiliation.** Even the highest opening is lower than a standing adult, so the bow is what the building asks of everybody — including whoever the highest opening was made for. It is the ume kbubu's two-sided check applied to a *set* of openings that differ from each other.
- **The counterexample is an ordinary courtesy**: raise the guests' opening so people carrying dishes can get through, and the gesture asked of everyone becomes a gesture asked only of those whose openings are lower. The building does not get worse; it starts saying something else.
- **The step between openings is the author's**, and the whole pack rests on it — the sources say the heights differ, not by how much. A test pins it against promotion.
- **Cloth stayed this pack's own** rather than joining the shared list: on the bade it is cladding bought by the metre and burned the same afternoon, here it is two pieces tied to a jamb.

**The thirty-second building is a Betawi rumah kebaya, and it is the first whose neighbours are not kin.** A brick plinth on low ground in Jakarta, a timber frame bought by the length, a tiled roof that folds once on its way down, and a terrace across the front facing the road. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 25 interpolated (86%).

- **Placed by a road rather than by a relationship.** Every other entry is sited by a compass rule, a family yard, a clan square, a river, a tree, or wherever a family stopped. This one stands on a plot beside a street, and **the line it may not build past was drawn by somebody who is not family** — the first constraint in this project that is somebody else's property. The counterexample widens the rooms, which nobody could object to, until the *eave* crosses the setback: what goes over a boundary first is a roof, which is exactly how the argument happens between neighbours.
- **A room for people who are not let in.** The langkan is reachable from the road without passing a door and the house's first door is behind it. Twenty-nine buildings before this divide space among people who belong to them; this one keeps a room for the people who do not.
- **The first building here made entirely of purchases** — timber sold by the length, tiles fired elsewhere, floor tiles by the piece, and **the only nails in the collection.** A sudung is built from what was standing around it an hour earlier; a khaim from a tree still alive underneath it. That is a note about a city, not about materials.
- **The third use of `swapXZ`, and the first on the stepped hip.** The fold shows from the side because the ridge lies parallel to the street.
- **`ridgeRise` was declared and read by nothing** — the ridge height follows from the two pitches — so it was deleted. A dimension the model does not use is a lie in the provenance table.

**The thirty-first building is a Sabu ammu hawu, and it is a boat without being one.** A house on Rai Hawu built and spoken of as a vessel: the ridge is a keel, the ends are a bow and a stern, the family are its crew, and it never floats. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 27 interpolated (87%).

- **The first check in this project that tests a likeness.** A tradition that calls its house a boat is making a claim, and the claim can be about words or about shape. `checkHullProportion` asks the second question — and **`test/sabu.test.ts` holds the declared range against the Bajau lepa's own beam ratio**, which is the first time one tradition's building has been measured against another's. It is a test rather than a check because a pack may not import a pack and a test may. Without the lepa, that range is a number the author chose so his own house would pass.
- **`checkEndsAreNotAlike` is the only check here whose success condition is that a symmetry fails.** Across the beam it is symmetric, as a hull is; along its length it must not be.
- **The roof is the wall.** The thatch comes down to a hand above the floor and the way in is the gap under the eave — so the eave height follows from the beam, and **widening the house closes its own door.** The likeness fails first and the door afterwards, which is the right order for what this pack is about.
- Lontar is the fifth roofing plant here, and the roof and what is stored under it come off the same tree. Seba is the southernmost site.

**The thirtieth entry is a Mentawai uma, and its central fact cannot be checked.** A long house on ironwood posts on Siberut: an open veranda facing the river, a closed room with one hearth per household, a second veranda behind. There is no chief. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 27 interpolated (87%).

- **`nobodyIsSenior` is canon and no invariant reports on it, deliberately.** A building that genuinely has no rank and a building whose rank nobody modelled are the same model. The pack checks what can be checked — equal shares, an open front, one record held in common — and says the rest is a claim rather than a verdict. **A test asserts that no check for it exists**, so that anybody who later writes one has to ask what it could be measuring. The Baduy prohibitions and the Buton ranked floors met this wall; this is the first entry with the uncheckable thing at its centre.
- **The rumah limas's section, with the opposite claim.** Both are graded front to back. There, where a guest is seated *is* their standing; here the grade follows what is being done rather than who is doing it. If two buildings of one shape can say opposite things, a shape alone is never enough to read one by.
- **The jaraik is the only record in the collection that belongs to nobody in particular** — against the Nias behu (one household's feast) and the Bugis timpa laja (one household's rank).
- **`checkFloorSpans` comes from what people do on the floor**: turuk is danced on the veranda, so the planks cross with nothing under the middle. The counterexample spaces the bearers further apart — exactly what a dancing floor wants — until a plank no longer crosses.

**The twenty-ninth entry is an Orang Rimba sudung, and it is the only building here that ends by being walked away from.** A single slope of leaf over a floor just off the forest floor at Bukit Duabelas, put up in an afternoon out of what is within reach of the spot. Fifteen parts, three joints, one material list of three things that were standing in that forest an hour earlier. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 20 interpolated (83%).

- **It ends on a day nobody chooses.** Melangun: when somebody dies the family leaves the place and does not return, and the shelter is left standing for the forest to finish. Three other buildings here end by *decision* — a bade burned on an appointed afternoon, a woloan house unpegged to be sold, a waruga cut so that it never moves. This is a fourth answer, not a version of any of them.
- **The first anthropometric figure here that sets a plan.** Four packs measure a body — a bale's standing owner, a waruga's folded dead, a body that must not fit through a bhaga's door, a body stooping into an ume kbubu — and all four measure a *height*. This measures people lying side by side, and it measures a width.
- **Bounded by an arm and an afternoon.** Nothing is sawn, hauled or bought, so the longest member is what somebody can cut nearby and carry. **The counterexample gives everyone more room to sleep** — the first thing a family of six would change — and the front pole passes what anybody can lift: the shelter that would be better to sleep in cannot be built where it is needed. The third limit of this kind, after the woloan's lorry and the Baduy's unspliceable pole, and the smallest.
- **`checkNothingIsFixed` is an absence with a reason.** No peg, no nail, no buried post, nothing bought: a building that must be walked away from cannot hold anything anybody would come back for.
- **Rattan is not in its material list**, though the whole thing is tied with it — the lashings are joints, and a joint is not a part. `test/materials.test.ts` said so, and it was right.
- **The thinnest-sourced pack in the collection, and it says so first.** No measured drawing of a sudung exists and probably never will. Its caution also states the thing a page like this can otherwise elide: the Orang Rimba are living people in a forest that is shrinking, and this is not a record of the past.

**The twenty-eighth building is an Atoni ume kbubu, and it is the only roof here that must keep something in.** Round, thatched to the ground, one door a person stoops through, no window at all, a fire on the floor and the seed maize hanging in the smoke above it. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 32 interpolated (89%).

- **A roof with a second job.** The other twenty-eight only have to keep water out. The smoke cures the seed and keeps the weevils out of it, so every hole this building does not have is the reason it works.
- **`checkLoftInTheSmoke` is a purpose check** — the third after the Sumbanese tower that has to hold something and the Korowai hearth that has to be able to fall. Where the loft sits follows from the framing; how far up the smoke stays useful is a declared band; nothing relates them. The counterexample raises the loft, which is the first thing anybody would do with a fire under a platform of dry maize, and **what fails is the crop rather than the building**.
- **The only two-sided check in the project.** The door must be taller than a stooping adult and lower than a standing one, and both bounds are the point.
- **A size taken from a length of time.** The loft's depth is how many harvests a household keeps against a bad year — every other size here comes from a body, a room, a rank, a household, a crowd, or what a building depicts.
- **And the lopo, in the same yard: round, on posts, no walls at all.** The same people build a thing that must not ventilate and a thing that is nothing but ventilation, a few metres apart. The honai and the mbaru niang made that point a thousand kilometres apart; this makes it in one yard. Soe is now the southernmost site.

**The twenty-seventh entry is the Ngada ngadhu and bhaga, and it is the first whose subject is a pair.** A carved post under a conical thatch cap and a little house on short posts, standing facing each other in the square at Bena: a clan's male ancestor and its female one. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 31 interpolated (89%).

- **Two objects of different kinds that have to exist together.** The tanean asked whether a `Built` can hold more than one building; this asks whether it must hold one *kind*, and it need not. `checkPairIsComplete` is the only invariant here whose subject is that two different things exist together — the baileo's says several things of one kind must be equal.
- **A house at a size nobody can enter.** A bhaga's dimensions come from what it *depicts*, which no other size in this project does. So the pack declares a body against the same `anthropometry` key the bale and the waruga use and requires the building to lose. **The counterexample opens the door** — the most reasonable change anybody could make — and a model of a house becomes a very small house. The third refutation ending in a sound building, and the first where what breaks is a category.
- **Neither half shelters anybody.** Written first as a headroom check, which was inventing a cramped building to make a point; the claim is about access and floor instead.
- **The site figure inverts the project:** the houses are the setting and the ancestors are the subject. It is the only site tagged `canon`, because the sources state the arrangement rather than the author placing it.
- **The planted third of the post is declared and not drawn.** The core refuses parts below y = 0 — right for the other twenty-eight — so a real dimension of the real object lives in the table instead. The first time that rule has met a building it disagrees with.

**The twenty-sixth building is a Buton malige, and it is the only one that gets wider as it rises.** Four storeys of timber inside the Keraton wall at Baubau, pegged and wedged with no iron anywhere, each storey projecting past the one below it on all four sides — so the largest floor in the building is its highest and the smallest is the one on the ground. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 24 interpolated (86%).

- **An assumption nobody had written down.** The core never required a plan to shrink with height; twenty-five packs simply did it. The Tobati kariwari is the only other building here with a named stack of floors and its levels get *smaller* upward, because the older age grades hold fewer people — `test/buton.test.ts` holds the two against each other, because an agreement that has never been contradicted is indistinguishable from a constraint.
- **Rank sets a cantilever.** The projection is carried on pale arms and how many a household may put up is its standing; a house entitled to none does not project at all and stands plumb, which is a different building rather than a smaller one. On a tongkonan rank multiplies size, on a joglo it stacks roofs, on a saoraja it stacks boards that carry nothing. This is the only pack where a social rule decides how far a building may hang outside its own footprint.
- **The limit lands at the top.** Every arm springs from the same frame, so what each has to span is the projection accumulated up to its level: the topmost arm is always the longest. The counterexample steps further out until that arm cannot reach — **so the household that overreaches loses exactly the floor it was overreaching for.** Writing the check against a single storey's oversail would have made it a check that never bites on the storey that matters.
- **`floorsAreRanked` is declared and not checked**, on the Baduy pack's precedent: a floor forbidden to somebody looks exactly like a floor that is not.
- **Two more of the recurring faults**, at a seventh and eighth building: an arm sitting flush under the beam it carries touches it on one plane and engages nothing, and a post standing on its stone does the same — both now overlap, and the pad stone grew a socket.

**The twenty-fifth entry is a Madurese tanean lanjang, and it is not a building.** A langgar closes the west end of a long yard, houses stand along the north side facing south, kitchens face them across it, and the eldest household stands westmost with each married daughter's house added eastward in the order she was born. Provenance: 0 measured / 4 canon / 28 interpolated (88%).

- **The gap the Bali pack named for itself, filled.** "A Balinese house is a compound and this models one building." Here the compound is the subject — and the registry took nine buildings without a word, because it never asked how many a `Built` contains. The whole generic half of the invariant suite ran over them as though they were one.
- **The yard is a part, and it is emitted first.** It is not space left between buildings; the buildings are arranged around it. `checkYardIsClear` is the plan equivalent of the Korowai check about the air under a floor — a void that is the point of the arrangement rather than what is left of it — and the eaves may oversail its edge, because that strip of shade is where people sit.
- **The stages are a family rather than a build.** Level the yard, raise the langgar, then one house per daughter as she marries. It is the only raising sequence in this project that shows decades.
- **Standing is stated twice and only one of them can go wrong.** Position cannot — a row is a row. Size can: the tonghuh's frontage and the daughters' are independent numbers, and the counterexample widens the daughters' houses until the row says the youngest outranks the eldest. **The second refutation here that ends with a perfectly sound building**, after the bale's.
- **It corrected an earlier pack.** The rumoh Aceh was described as the only building here turned by a rule from outside the archipelago; a langgar at the head of a tanean stands where it does for exactly that reason. Aceh now claims the true, narrower thing — first, and the only one whose *whole plan* is turned by it — and a test in the Madura pack holds both packs to it.
- **`SceneModel` describes only part of its subject for the first time**, and `madura/scene.ts` says which fields are the cluster's and which are one house's rather than bending a field until it lies. No symmetry is claimed along the yard, for the second time after the betang.

**The twenty-fourth building is a Korowai khaim, and it is the first whose structure is alive.** A house high above the forest floor in the Becking and Dairam headwaters, carried by a wanbon topped off at floor height with its roots still in the ground. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 26 interpolated (84%).

- **Part of it is still growing.** Twenty-three buildings stand on something dead — stone, piles, masonry, a hillside, a keel, people's shoulders. The core's assumption that a part, once placed, stays as placed had never been tested until this one.
- **The first check whose two numbers belong to different parties.** The household picks the height; the tree decides what is left of the trunk up there. `checkTrunkCarries` reports **skipped** when the house stands on cut poles, because with no trunk nothing varies with height and a version written against a constant pole section would compare two constants — the fault this project has made four times.
- **The empty air under the floor is the argument**, so it is checked: nothing may be under this house at all. `underfloorHeight` reports what the field was named for on its eleventh meaning, and by a wide margin the largest figure it has carried.
- **A fire that can be dropped.** The hearths hang in openings so they can be cut loose and fall to the ground; the floor has to be *open* under every fire, which is why the deck is four strips to a bay rather than one sheet. **The only check here whose subject is a part leaving the building.**
- **One partition, two sides**, each with its own hearth and its own ladder — the Karo house's question answered the opposite way. **Bark and earth moved to the shared material list**, and the earth is the better move: the Baduy hillside and a Korowai hearth are one substance doing opposite jobs.
- **The caution says the famous numbers are the ones to distrust:** everyday houses are a few metres up, and some of the tallest were built for a camera.

**The twenty-third building is a Balinese bade, and it is the only one made in order to be destroyed.** It is the tower a body is carried to the cremation ground on: built in weeks out of bamboo, timber, cloth and paper, lifted onto the shoulders of twenty, forty or eighty people, carried down the road, turned about at every crossroads so the spirit cannot find its way home, and burned. Nothing of it exists the next morning. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 17 interpolated (77%), 100% interpolated by part.

- **It has no foundation, and the zero is the whole pack.** `checkCarriedNotFounded` is a check for an absence: nothing buried, nothing set on stone, nothing reaching below the carrying lattice. Twenty-two buildings here bear on stone, piles, masonry, a hillside or a keel; this one bears on shoulders — **this building's foundation walks, and turns at every crossroads.**
- **Its plan is a headcount of the living.** Every other plan here comes from a room, a rank, a body or a household. The side of the lattice is how many people can get underneath it at once, which is the only dimension in the project taken from a crowd rather than from an occupant.
- **The fourth "check that restates its own input", and it took the same shape as the other three.** `checkOverTheBearers` was first written as the distance of the centre of all the parts from the middle of the lattice — zero at every rule combination, because the tower is symmetric about both axes. What varies is *slenderness*, and its two numbers are independent: the lattice comes from a headcount and the height from a tier count, with nothing tying them together. `test/bade.test.ts` asserts the reported number moves when a rule moves, which is the test that would have caught it.
- **The counterexample fails at the crowd rather than at the building.** Raise each tier and no member fails, nothing overhangs, the stack still narrows. What runs out is the base: past a point the weight is too far above the people holding it, and the tower that says the most is the one that cannot be carried down the road it was built for.
- **Tiers that shelter nothing.** On a joglo the tumpang are a roof over a room and the rank is carried by what it covers. Here there is air underneath, so the count is pure statement — and it is stated in something built not to last. Its rungs are odd for a rank reason, deliberately not a parity rule, so the rumoh Aceh's claim to the only parity rule here stays true.
- **The exact inverse of the waruga, and the two are one argument.** One material that lasts against four that burn; a building that will never move against one carried once; a tomb that can be measured any morning against a tower nobody could measure without standing in a cremation. Two buildings for the dead, made two buildings apart, and neither is a version of the other.
- **`underfloorHeight` reports the depth of the lattice — the tenth meaning that field has carried, and the first with no space under it at all.** What is under this building is people. And the site figure is a *route*, not a place: a crossroads, the only setting in the collection the building does not stay in.
- **Cloth and gold paper are the first surfaces here that are not building materials.** Everything else in this project is quarried, felled, split, fired or cut. These are bought by the metre and would be ruined by one night of rain, which is exactly the point.

**The twenty-second building is a Minahasa waruga, and it is the first that is not for the living.** A stone tomb: a box with a chamber and a roof-shaped lid, the dead placed inside seated and facing north, further members of the family added to the same box over generations. Nobody enters it and it is used for nothing. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 13 interpolated (72%).

- **The registry never asked whether anybody lives in a building**, which is why it took this one without a single exception — the strongest evidence yet that the neutral contract is neutral about the right things.
- **One material, the shortest list in the project.** Box and lid out of one block: no timber, no lashing, no iron, and one joint, which is the lid sitting in its rebate and is also the building's only opening. The exact opposite of the rumah woloan, which the same people build to be taken apart and carried away.
- **A body measured seated and folded**, against the same `anthropometry` source key the Balinese bale uses for a living, standing body. The same principle on an entirely different occasion, and both packs' body figures file together and apart from everything else.
- **Its counterexample runs into the size of a block**, pushed there by the one thing nobody can plan for: how many of a family there turn out to be. The stone is chosen on the day the first of them dies.
- **Its carving is the largest named absence in the collection.** The face records what the person did in life — fisherman, midwife, soldier, teacher — and that record is why these stones are still readable. Not modelled, on the policy every other pack states, and it weighs more here than anywhere else.

**The twenty-first building is a Bajau lepa, and it is the first with no ground under it.** A houseboat: the family lives aboard, the hearth is a box of sand because there is no earth to build one on, and the address of the house is wherever it is moored. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 28 interpolated (85%).

- **The hull comes off `sweepSurface` — the roof primitive.** A hull is a section swept along a curve with a knee at the bilge, which is the same arithmetic as a roof and needed nothing added. Six houses sweep a roof with it; this one sweeps a boat, and the primitive did not notice.
- **The first balance check in the project**, and it is about weight being *low* rather than inside a footprint — the bade's, two buildings later, is the second and asks the opposite question. Both limits are the author's and both say so: this project has no material properties, so neither check can say a thing floats or stands, only where the parts are.

**The twentieth building is a rumoh Aceh, and it is the only one turned by a rule from outside the archipelago.** It lies east–west because prayer is toward the west. Nineteen others are turned by something visible from their own yard — a compass rule of their own, a granary, a river, a road, a stone, the root of a tree, the fall of a hillside. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 25 interpolated (83%).

- **The pack states the reason and the check states the axis, and they are kept apart.** Nothing in a model knows which way Mecca is; what a model can hold is which axis is long and that the ridge follows it. The caution says the qibla from Aceh is actually about 293°, and that this models the east–west axis the sources give rather than a computed bearing.
- **The only parity rule in the project, and the smallest counterexample.** The ladder's treads must be odd — and the count is *derived* (floor height over tread rise), so nothing in that arithmetic knows about odd and even. A centimetre on the rise flips nine to eight: the house does not move, and the difference between the sound one and the broken one is one piece of wood. A declared count would have made the check a restatement, the fault caught four times before.
- **The rumah limas's member, saying the opposite thing.** Three parts across the width with the middle one raised — but what is lifted is the room a guest does *not* enter, so the height states a boundary rather than a rank.
- **A third answer to moving ground.** No iron: beams threaded through mortises cut clean through the posts, pegged and lashed. Nias triangulates every bay, Arfak braces nothing, this one is simply not forced to be stiff.

**The nineteenth building is a Baduy imah, and it is the first whose rules are prohibitions.** The pikukuh say what may not be done: the ground may not be cut, timber may not be sawn, iron may not enter the frame. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 27 interpolated (84%).

- **The only building whose part list contains the ground.** Eighteen stand on a datum because a model needs somewhere to start; this one stands on a tilted slab of earth that is *in the model* because it is the one thing the builders may not change. The core took a sloping site without noticing — a stone resting on a hillside is a stone resting on something.
- **The stones sit where they lie, so every post is a different length while the floor is one level plane.** The difference between the longest post and the shortest is the slope, stated in metres of timber — a number that is zero by construction everywhere else here.
- **The first counterexample where the rule defeats itself.** Steepen the ground and everything the builders do stays correct, until the downhill post is longer than one pole and a post is not spliced. The only way out is to cut the hillside, which is the prohibition.
- **Two prohibitions are declared and tested by nothing, and the pack says so.** A sawn beam and a split one are the same shape. A pack that wrote down only the rules it could check would be quietly editing the tradition to fit the software.

**The eighteenth building is a Karo siwaluh jabu, and it completes a set of three.** Eight households in one room with no partition anywhere, and four hearths each shared by two. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 28 interpolated (85%).

- **Three buildings, one social fact, three answers that share no member.** The betang gives each household an identical room and lengthens by one each time; the baileo makes every clan's seat equal by rule; this one divides nothing, and standing is stated by position — the only marker here that can neither lie nor be taken down, because removing it means removing the room.
- **The order is set by the timber.** The great beams are laid root-end first, and the household at that end is senior. The only rule in the project whose datum is a property of the *material* — and reversing it leaves the model byte-identical apart from which household is called senior, so it needs a check rather than a comment.
- **`checkHearthClearance` exists only because there are no partitions.** In a betang each fire is inside its own bilik; here what keeps it off a post is a declared distance. The counterexample grows the fire until it reaches the frame: this building is dangerous for exactly the reason it is interesting.
- **`Stepper` gained a step size**, because a Karo hearth is shared by two and five households is not a smaller version of six.

**The seventeenth building is a Minahasa rumah woloan, and it is the only one that has to come apart.** Around Woloan and Tomohon a timber house is a commodity: built to be sold whole, numbered, unpegged, carried away by road and re-erected somewhere else. Sixteen buildings here are raised where they will stand and stay there, and taking one down means destroying it. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 25 interpolated (83%).

- **`checkCanBeUnbuilt` is a kind of check this project had never had.** Every pack asks whether the building can go *up* — each part, when placed, finds something under it. This one asks the same sequence backwards: each part, when removed, may not still be carrying anything. **A house that can only be demolished passes the forward check and fails this one**, and until this building existed nothing here could tell the two apart. It caught the veranda deck being laid before its own posts, which forwards looked fine because the deck also touches the body.
- **The haul length is the only figure in the project that is about a journey.** Not the building, not the people in it, not the place: how long a piece a lorry takes. The frame is a set of pieces cut to that, junctions on bay lines so they can be numbered. The counterexample widens the house until the bearers will not go — better by every ordinary measure, and no longer able to leave.
- **One joint kind, and it is not a shortfall.** A peg is a joint that can be taken out again; nothing is nailed, glued or notched so that undoing it breaks it.
- **The pad stones are the one part in the collection that deliberately does not travel.** When the house goes, what is left on the site is a plan drawn in stone.
- **The site figure is a building in transit** — the numbered bundles of another house stacked in the yard, waiting for the road. The only setting here that is not a place.

**The sixteenth building is a Tobati-Enggros kariwari, and it is the first that does not stand on land.** It stands in Youtefa Bay on posts driven into the bed, eight-sided, under a tall cone. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 24 interpolated (83%).

- **No ground, and the consequences differ in kind rather than degree.** There is no pad stone under any post, because there is nowhere to set one; the height of the floor answers a number that changes twice a day. In this pack **y = 0 is the bed of a bay** and the water is a sheet lying on it, which the core took without noticing — a post standing on the bed is a post standing on something.
- **The levels are ages, so the section is a biography.** Boys below, young men above them, elders at the top; each floor smaller than the one below because the older grades hold fewer people; one pole between each consecutive pair and none that skips. It is the only vertical division here a person passes *through* rather than belongs to.
- **`checkAboveTheTide` was a restatement, the fourth time.** The floor was defined as sitting a freeboard above the water, so no value of any dimension could flood it. How high to cut the posts is a decision and whether the sea reaches the floor is not — two numbers now, and the counterexample is **the first here defeated by something nobody controls**.
- **Two rules where every other pack has three, and the shortfall is the finding.** What the sources record is the number of levels and the walkway. A third rule would be inventing a variation nobody has built, which is worse than inventing a metre.
- **`coneSurface` asked for eight facets makes an octagonal pyramid**, and needed nothing — the same evidence the bale gave for `steppedHip`.

**The fifteenth building is a Maluku baileo, and it is the first that belongs to nobody.** The negeri's own house: raised, wall-less, where the saniri meets and the ancestors are addressed. Nobody sleeps in it and no household owns it. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 30 interpolated (86%).

- **The tally counts a community.** A rank multiplies, a body measures, a household tally lengthens a house. Here the number is how many clans make up the village — and a soa does not live in the baileo, it *sits* in it. One bay of floor, one pair of posts and one seat each.
- **`checkPlacesAreEqual` is the first invariant here whose subject is that two parts are the *same*.** It is the exact inverse of the Palembang kekijing: there the floor steps and where a guest is seated is their standing; here the floor refuses to step, and the refusal is the statement.
- **`checkOpenOnAllSides` is the first that measures a building against a person inside it.** `seatedEye` is a dimension of a human being: it moves nothing in the model and decides whether the model passes. A knee-high screen is allowed; a chest-high one ends the sight line and with it the reason the building has no walls.
- **The stone is a rule, because the sources record both arrangements.** In front, nothing reaches over it; inside, the floor is opened around it — and the first draft put it at the middle of the building where a bearer runs, which the check refused. It stands on a joint between bays now.
- **Sago leaf moved to `SHARED_MATERIALS`**, the fourth key to go that way.

**The fourteenth building is a Banjar rumah bubungan tinggi, and it is the first here that is a sequence of roofs.** Every other building in this project has one roof — swept, hipped, coned, domed, hooded, towered — over the whole plan. This one has four in a row along a single ridge: the pelatar open at the front where the house meets the river, the surambi under a low shed, the palidangan under its own tall form, the padu under a shed again. You do not read it from its plan. You read it by walking the ridge and naming what changes overhead. Provenance: 0 measured / 5 canon / 28 interpolated (85%), 100% interpolated by part.

- **A rule that selects a geometric primitive.** Every other rule in this project sets a number, picks a name, counts something, or turns a part on. `jenis` chooses what shape stands over the core — a steep gable, a hipped roof, a lower gable — and the house takes its *name* from the answer: bubungan tinggi, palimasan, gajah baliku, otherwise the same building. It is the only rule of its kind here.
- **The one counterexample that ends with a sound building.** Thirteen others end with something that cannot be constructed, or is dead, or has stopped being what it was for. Lower this ridge and nothing fails — the chain still meets, every segment still has its roof, the shingles still lap, the floors still step. What stops being true is the *name*.
- **A table of constants beside the table of dimensions is the provenance layer being escaped quietly.** `JENIS` held `rise: DIMS.tinggiRise.value`, a copy taken at import time. `withDimValue` swaps the value in the pack, so the sensitivity probe and the counterexample search were moving a number nothing read, and the counterexample could not break its own check at any factor. The table now holds the dimension *key*.
- **Two more of the recurring faults, at a fifth and a sixth building.** The ridges were emitted before the rafters carrying them — a roof written top-down because that is how it is drawn. And the platform's rafters were set out over the front overhang, where there is no plate: sticks hanging in air, the rumah gadang's cantilever fault again. The overhang is carried by the ridge, which is why the ridge is longer than the run of rafters under it.
- **The floors step and they mean the opposite of the Palembang limas's steps.** There, where a guest is seated is their standing and the steps *are* the statement. Here they follow the roofs and the water off a house standing in a tidal swamp — so the check is written as a fall away from the core in both directions rather than one staircase front to back, because writing it the other way would have imported the limas's claim wholesale.
- **`swapXZ` moved to `lib/core/geometry.ts` and the axis flag stayed put.** `minang/roof.ts` said a third house turning its sweep would move the axis into `SweepOptions`. This is not that: what was needed was the turn itself, on a *different* primitive. One house still sweeps on Z.
- **Nothing of its own in the material set, for a reason worth stating.** Ulin and its shingles are the betang's, off the same generators — the same forest, the same island, the same water. The key moved to `SHARED_MATERIALS`, which is exactly what that list is for.
- **One simplification, stated in the caution rather than hidden.** The front and back segments are very low gables, not true single-slope sheds, because the primitive is symmetric about its ridge and a shed is not. The sequence of heights is right; one of the four forms is an approximation.

**The thirteenth building is a Dani honai, and it is the only one here whose problem is cold.** The Baliem valley is at sixteen hundred metres on the equator, where the days are mild and the nights are not, and a honai is a device for holding a fire's heat until morning. Everything follows: small, because a small volume is cheaper to warm; round, because a circle encloses the most floor for the least wall; low, because heat rises; no window at all; a door that makes a person stoop; and people sleeping in a loft above the fire. The other twelve answer rain, a moving ground, rot, rats, or standing.

- **`coneSurface` moved to `lib/core/cone.ts`**, on the condition `manggarai/cone.ts` set for itself: a second house is round. Nothing in it knew about five stacked stores or a fifteen-metre point. **And the two together settle something**: both are round, both thatched to the ground, both off the same primitive — and one is forty times the volume of the other. **Roundness says nothing on its own.**
- **The checks test form, never warmth, and say so.** This project has no material properties and will not acquire any, so `checkSmallVolume` measures the room against the mbaru niang's rather than against a threshold nobody could justify. Exactly the limit the Nias pack states about its triangles.
- **`underfloorHeight` is zero for the first time, and the zero is a decision.** Twelve buildings stand on posts or masonry; this one sits on the earth because the ground holds heat. `registry.test.ts` asserted the field was positive — true of twelve, false of the thirteenth — which is **the third test in this project over-fitted to the examples it was written against**. It now asserts what it means.
- **Two more "interpolated where it should be derived" faults**, making seven. The loft was a share of the radius and floated clear of the wall ring that carries it; and the roof outline started at the overhang's tip *level with the wall*, so no rafter ever passed over a post and all twenty joints engaged nothing.
- **`checkJointStages` forced the build order to be right.** A rafter resting on a wall post has to follow it immediately, and with the door and the loft between them it did not. The door frame is set before the wall and the loft fitted after the roof — which is how the work actually goes.

**The twelfth building is a Sasak lumbung, and it is the first that is not a house.** Nobody lives in it, there is no storey a person could stand up in, and in a Sasak yard it is routinely the most carefully made thing standing. That inverts an assumption the other eleven share without stating it: **that the care in a building tracks the standing of the people inside**. Here it tracks the value of what is stored, and the people sit underneath, in the part not built for them.

- **Its defining detail is aimed at an animal.** Each post carries a broad disc just below the floor whose only job is to stop a rat, and it is the only element in the project directed at something other than a person. `checkRatGuard` and `checkNoOtherWayUp` are a pair and only the pair says anything: a disc is not a defence, a disc *with nothing else reaching from the ground to the floor* is. The second check's subject is the absence of a route.
- **A curve is many steps.** The hood is `steppedHip` with nine levels instead of two — a sixth form from one primitive, and no new primitive was needed. `checkHoodCurves` is what makes that honest: what separates a curve from a faceted cone is that the pitch keeps changing, which a check that only counted levels would not know.
- **`checkRatGuard` was a restatement, the third time this has happened.** The disc's radius was derived *from* the post, so the overhang was constant by construction and the check could not fail. A rat guard is a plank cut to a size and the post through it is whatever it is — so the overhang is a difference between two independent numbers, and a stouter post eats it from the inside. That is now the counterexample, and its direction is the point: **the thing that defeats this defence is the first improvement a builder would reach for.**
- **The render caught what nothing else could.** The hood covered the discs completely — the one detail the building turns on, invisible behind its own thatch. And it is not only a viewing problem: a skirt hanging past a guard hands back the route the guard exists to close. `checkNoOtherWayUp` now requires the eave to stop above them.
- **It uses more Indonesian than any other pack, deliberately.** Only `lumbung` is used as a local term; the guard and the parts of the hood have Sasak names the author is not confident enough of to print. The policy the joglo set, applied widest where the sourcing is thinnest.

**The eleventh house is the Arfak rumah kaki seribu, and it is the exact opposite of the sixth.** The Nias omo answers a moving ground by triangulating every bay of its substructure, because a rectangle racks and a triangle does not. This one answers the same problem by bracing *nothing at all*: hundreds of small poles, each leaning its own way, tied to no neighbour and buried in no earth, so the whole frame moves with the ground. `checkNothingIsBraced` is the literal negation of `checkBracing`.

**That pairing is the strongest result eleven houses have produced.** The Nias pack had to narrow this project's premise to admit rules about the earth alongside rules about people. This one shows the narrowing was enough — because a rule about the earth constrains a form no more tightly than a rule about people does. Two houses, one problem, two opposite answers, both standing. Neither is a lesser version of the other, and it took both being here to see it.

- **The only house with a single joint kind, and that is a position rather than a shortfall.** Everything is lashed; a lashing can work a little without breaking, where a peg or a notch forces two members to behave as one.
- **The lean is a hash of each leg's own grid position.** Poles leaning every which way is the whole character of the substructure and the obvious way to get it is `Math.random`, which `lib/` forbids — a house that came out a different shape on every load would be a screensaver, and two readers comparing screens would not be comparing the same building.
- **It is the thinnest-sourced pack here and says so at the front.** Its canon list states only what the Depdikbud survey states plainly; *every* metric figure is the author's, including the leg count, which is the single best-known thing about the building. A test pins that they all stay `interpolated` against `none`.
- **The foot wanders, not the head.** The legs were leaned at the top first, which put each head up to 130 mm off a bearer 100 mm wide, so the lashings engaged nothing. A pole is set out where it must land and its foot goes wherever the ground allows.
- **`checkFrameSymmetry` was asserting the wrong axis.** Written about z = 0, which for a ridge running along Z is a claim about the two *ends* — and they are not alike, because the door is at one of them. The mirror plane is the one the ridge lies in.

**The tenth house is the Bugis saoraja, and it is the first whose rank holds nothing up.** A tongkonan's rank multiplies every dimension; a joglo's tier count grows the roof carrying it; a rumah limas builds standing into the floor people walk on. In all of those the claim and the structure are the same material — you cannot remove the statement without taking the building apart. The timpa laja is a stack of boards on the gable face whose count is the household's rank, and they carry no load at all.

- **So it is the first house here whose rank marker can lie**, and that is the point rather than a curiosity: a household could put up more boards than it was entitled to, and its neighbours could count them from the road. `checkRankCarriesNothing` is the only invariant in the project whose subject is what a part does *not* do — and it tests it by **rebuilding the house without every board and running the structural checks again**, which is stronger than inspecting the boards' joints, because a part can be declared jointless and still be the only thing under a rafter.
- **The only bound in the project that refuses a number the building could carry.** A bola holds nine boards without noticing; what stops it is that nine is not its to claim. The rail says so in those words rather than silently clamping.
- **Its counterexample is the first that is purely rhetorical.** Raise the boards and the frame, the roof, the three worlds and every clearance are untouched — the building would stand for a century. What breaks is that the stack climbs off the gable it is riding: a claim made taller so it can be read from further away, with no triangle left to sit on. Ten houses, ten rules that cannot be carried out, and this is the one where nothing physical gives way.
- **A board nailed to a gable is carried by that gable.** The boards were first placed a gap in front of the roof, touching nothing, and the build-order check refused them — correctly. There is no separate projection dimension now: the board lies flat on the thatch and its thickness is its projection.

**The ninth house is the Palembang rumah limas, and it is the first where the floor is the hierarchy.** One room whose floor rises in named steps — the kekijing — from the street to the family, and where a guest is seated on that sequence is their standing. The social fact is not something a dimension is derived from; **it is a height, in metres**. Nobody is refused entry; they are seated lower.

- **It is also the first house that separates its two plan axes.** The depth carries the sequence and grows only by adding a kekijing; the width is only size. `checkAxesAreIndependent` rebuilds across both rules and asserts neither moves the other — a claim no earlier pack could make, because in all eight a plan is one thing. A model that lengthened the guest list by widening the house would look entirely reasonable and be saying something false.
- **Its counterexample is the first where the social claim is the thing that holds.** Steepen the steps and the sequence stays perfectly ordered and correctly named; what runs out is the air over the top of it, because the roof does not step with the floor. A household insisting on ever sharper distinctions eventually cannot stand up in its own gegajah. Nine houses, nine rules that cannot be carried out — and here it is the building that gives way, not the rule.
- **`checkHeadroom` was a restatement until the wall was measured from the right place.** Written with the wall rising from the *topmost* level, headroom there is exactly the wall height and the check can never fail. A wall is one board from the lowest floor to the plate; measured that way, the top level's headroom is a comparison of two independent numbers and the check bites.
- **`zones` deliberately does not fit, and this time the numbers would have.** Five heights, one above the next — the field would take them. But they are five parts of one room, and a reader shown five bands would take them for storeys like the mbaru niang's. The bands are the three real storeys; the sequence is in the copy. **A field that fits the numbers and misleads about the meaning is worse than one that visibly does not fit.**
- **A `choice` field writes a string.** `kekijing` was declared as a choice over `['3','5']`, which type-checks — and `normaliseRules` compared against the number 3, so every three-step house read back from its own address came out with five. The codec quietly giving a household two distinctions it had not claimed.

**The eighth house is the Sumbanese uma, and it is the first whose roof is not a shelter.** On the other seven the roof covers something and its size follows from what is underneath. Here a tower of thatch rises several times the height of the house, and it is there because of what is *in* it — the uma deta, the loft where the marapu are kept. The building is not a house with a tall roof; it is a container with a house around its foot, and the raising sequence says so: the loft goes in before the tower.

- **`checkTowerHoldsSomething` is the only check in the project that tests a purpose rather than a form.** A tower with nothing in it is a tall roof, which is a different building. **`checkLoftBeforeTower` asserts an order rather than a geometry** — reverse it and the model is byte-identical; what changes is what the sequence says the building is for, which is exactly why it needs a check rather than a comment.
- **The sharpest either/or in the project.** Every other flag adds a part or changes a proportion. An uma kamadungu keeps no marapu, so it builds no peak — it is not a smaller uma mbatangu, it is a different kind of object.
- **The most consequential number here is among the least supported, and that stays visible.** `menaraRise` sets the silhouette a photograph of Sumba is recognisable by, and it is `interpolated` against `none`: the sources agree the peak is tall and that taller says more, and none gives a figure. A test asserts it is not quietly upgraded. A pack whose boldest number is its softest is what the bar exists to reveal.
- **Two more "interpolated where it should be derived" faults, making four across four houses.** The shoulder was a fraction of the eave, which stood the tower *inboard of the four posts carrying it* — a container with its legs outside it. And the loft was a fraction of the way up the tower, which put a platform in mid-air. Both are derived now: the shoulder is the square the kambaniru make, and the uma deta is the tower's floor, not a level inside it. After the joglo's roof levels and the omo's loft, this is a pattern worth naming — **when a thing rests on another thing, take its position from what it rests on.**
- **`Layout.window` → `bukaan` in the omo, and here the rule carries tenths.** `menara` is the only ratio rule in the project and the codec has no fractional field; storing tenths and dividing at the point of use is a smaller change than a new field kind, and does not make seven other traditions pay for this one's arithmetic.

**The seventh house is the Dayak betang, and it is the first with no characteristic size.** Every other building here has a shape its rules *size*; this one has a shape its rules *count*. Each household adds one bilik and one stretch of gallery to the end, so the length is a census — forty metres or two hundred, with no proportion governing the difference. `checkNoCharacteristicLength` states that, and it is the only invariant in this project that **passes by showing a proportion failing to hold**: from three households to twenty the length-to-width ratio runs 5 : 1 to 8 : 1 and never settles, while the width, the floor height and the ridge height do not move at all.

- **A survey would pin the share and not the house**, which is new for the provenance layer. Everywhere else, measuring the building settles it. Here every figure could become `measured` and the length would still be unknown, because the length is not a property of the building type. That is the correct answer rather than a gap, and `sensitivity.ts` says so.
- **No symmetry claim is made along the length, and the pack says why.** A house grown from one end is genuinely not symmetric, and picking an axis that happens to pass would state something untrue. `checkSectionIsConstant` replaces it with the regularity this building actually has: every share equal, and always one fewer partition than households.
- **A gable out of `steppedHip`, by giving both levels the same half-length.** Third distinct form from one primitive — stepped hip, pyramid, gable — and the primitive needed to know nothing. That is now settled.
- **Two of this pack's checks are also underivable-by-dimension**, like the omo's bracing, so the pattern is real rather than an accident: a check reading the same list the geometry was built from cannot be falsified by a number. Both are tested against their own arithmetic instead, and the counterexample route shows `checkShingleCoverage` against `shingleLap` — a shingle roof leaks at its joints, so take the lap away and the same ironwood over the same rafters stops covering itself.
- **The hejot is joined to nothing, and that is the fact.** A joint was written for it and `checkJointStages` refused it: a member placed last, resting on something built four stages earlier, is not engaged with it. So there is no third joint kind here — the way up leans, and at night it is pulled in.
- **The rafters were resting on whatever happened to be beneath them.** Six of them fell over doorways, where a bilik's front wall is not there, and the build-order check found it at once. They land on a plate now, and the plate on the gallery side is carried on posts continuing up from the floor — the open gallery stated in structure rather than in copy.

**The sixth house is the Nias omo, and it is the first whose governing rule comes from the ground rather than from people.** South Nias is on an active margin, so the understorey carries driwa on the diagonal as well as ehomo on the vertical: every bay of the substructure is a triangle, because a rectangle of four posts racks and a triangle does not. That makes it the first building here that is not orthogonal post-and-beam, and the first real test of this project's premise.

**The premise survived and had to be narrowed, which is the finding.** "A social fact becomes a dimension" was never the whole story: it is that *the rules a tradition states about its own building become dimensions*, and some of those rules are about people while others are about the earth. `everyBayTriangulated` is canon and is a claim about seismicity, so this pack's canon list does two jobs at once and the dimension notes say which is which. The five earlier houses could not have shown that, because none of them has a rule of the second kind.

- **`checkBracing` cannot be broken by pushing any dimension, and that is not a defect.** The braces are emitted from `layout.cells` and the check walks `layout.cells`, so widening a bay lengthens its diagonal for ever. That is the one-description discipline working. But it means the strongest structural claim in the pack is one no *number* can falsify, so it is falsified in `test/nias.test.ts` instead — by building a house and taking one plane of bracing away, by removing a single brace, and by shrinking one until it no longer reaches its corners. The counterexample route shows `checkRoofDominates` instead and says why. **A check nothing has ever seen fail is indistinguishable from one that cannot.**
- **A bay astride the mirror plane takes a cross, not a lean.** An even number of post columns puts one bay across z = 0, and a single leaning diagonal there cannot be symmetric — its own mirror leans the other way. That is a fact about the building, not a bug: the central bay gets both diagonals. It is why the brace count is not the cell count.
- **The behu are the first thing a rule adds *outside* the building.** Only a si'ulu raises them and each stone records a feast, so they state something about a household that no part of the house states. `checkFrameSymmetry` is scoped to exclude them, and `checkBracingVisible` had to learn the difference between a stone in the yard and something screening the understorey.
- **The loft was interpolated and floated; now it is derived and lands.** Sized as a share of the body it sat inside the rafters touching nothing, exactly as the joglo's roof levels did before they were taken from the pillar rings. Read off the hip at its own height, it lands on the frame that carries it and one invented number disappears.
- **`Layout.window` is now `Layout.bukaan`**, because `layout.window.height` reads exactly like DOM access to the architecture guard. The guard's value is that it is blunt; a field called `window` in a module that must never touch the DOM is a trap left for the next person.

**The fifth house is the Balinese bale, and it is here for its units rather than its form.** It is the plainest object in the project — a low platform, some posts, a hipped roof — and the reason to build it is that under Asta Kosala Kosali every principal length is a whole number of a measure of *its owner's body*: depa, hasta, musti. The other four traditions each let a household pick among fixed numbers and the metre stays neutral; here the unit is the social fact, and two households of identical standing build different buildings because they are different sizes. `lib/tradition/bali/module.ts` is the house. Three consequences worth keeping:

- **The provenance question splits in two, for the first time.** How many units (canon, where a source states the relation) and how long a unit is (anthropometry, and the author's). Those must never be merged into one metre figure with one class — the same rule that keeps two houses' interpolated shares apart, one level down. The `anthropometry` source key exists so that "not from a book about Bali" is visible in the table rather than hidden inside `none`, and a test asserts the two stay distinguishable.
- **`checkPengurip` passes by finding an inexactness.** A principal measure that lands exactly on its module is `mati` — dead — so every one carries a small increment and the house is required *not* to be exactly its own rule. Turning the rule off inverts the claim and the check then demands exactness, which is how you watch it refuse. Its counterexample is the only one in the project that ends with a building which stands up perfectly well: grow the increment until it is a whole unit and the house is dead again, one unit larger, wrong for a reason no amount of looking would reveal.
- **`checkModule` is the only check here that can see a hardcoded metre.** The provenance bar counts declarations; a number written into a builder is invisible to it. This one sees the arithmetic — and its companion test greps the builders for a unit count written inline, because `stockLength(s, 4, 'musti')` is a real dimension that `/sumber` would never list. Four were sitting in the first draft.

**`steppedHip` moved to `lib/core/hip.ts`, as `jawa/hip.ts` said it would when a second house hipped.** The bale hands it two levels — one eave, one ridge — and asks for a single band, and it needed nothing. That degenerate case is better evidence than a second elaborate one: a primitive that only works at the complexity it was written for is not a primitive. `partPoints` also moved into `lib/core/invariants.ts`, beside `partBounds`, because a second copy of an Euler matrix is a second thing to get wrong.

**A render is part of the gate, and it caught what the suite could not.** All sixteen Minang checks passed on a roof that was a long shed with six rods standing on its ridge, because none of them asked the question that mattered — whether the roof *surface* is what rises into the point. A gonjong is the end of the roof's own edge, lifted past the ridge; the hollow between a pair is the low ridge end between two high tips. That is now how it is built and there are two checks for it (`checkGonjongCount` requires the boarding to reach every tip; `checkEaveRises` requires the edge to hold its line over the middle, climb past the ridge, and take enough length doing it to be a curve). It took three looks. The second caught a sweep confined to the 0.9 m overhang — ten metres of rise over nine hundred millimetres, which rendered as four flat sails welded to the ends. Moving the lift deep inside the body fixed the slope and broke the other end of it: by the time the roof reached the end wall its edge had met its own ridge, so there was no gable left, and the carved panel closing it was still being cut to the level eave — projecting a metre past the roof each side and hanging four and a half metres below its edge. The lift now happens almost entirely over a deep overhang, `stationAt` is the single description of the roof's section, and everything reads it. The fourth look found the rafters over the overhang doing the same thing the gable panel had — cut to the level eave, half a metre outboard of the roof and a metre and a half below its edge, a set of loose sticks poking out of both ends.

The pattern in all four: **two places computing the same shape, and only one of them updated.** `checkRoofFollowsSection` is the general form of the answer — no member positioned at a station may reach outboard of that station's edge or hang below it by more than the depth of its own timber. A check written for one member would have caught one member. The same look found the rumah gadang wearing pa'barre allo across its whole front, because the material sets were split by key and the *construction* behind the key was left shared. CLAUDE.md already said the renderer's gate is the invariant suite **plus a human looking at it**. It meant it.

**A fifth instance, and the plainest yet: the mbaru niang's door was built behind its own roof.** The frame was set at the cone's radius so it would satisfy `checkInsideCone`, the thatch stands 0.20 m outside the cone at every height, and `buildThatch` emitted closed rings — so all three timbers were buried and there was no opening anywhere in the building. `checkOneDoor` passed throughout, because it counted two jambs and a lintel and computed a bearing; it never asked whether anything was open. `coneSurface` now takes a `gap` — a surface of revolution has no way to be interrupted and a door is exactly an interruption — the frame leans with the wall so it is not buried at one end whichever end you set it by, and `doorOpening()` is the one place that decides where the door is, so the frame stands on the numbers the thatch is cut by. The check now fails if any thatch vertex crosses the doorway or if a jamb sits inside the cone. The opening is wider than `doorWidth` because it has to clear the frame, and the check says so rather than printing the nominal figure.

Known gaps, all deliberate and none of them hidden:

- **Carving is texture-level, in both houses.** The pa'barre allo is constructed from its rule rather than traced, but it is drawn onto a canvas, not extruded. Relief casting real shadow is the target.
- **Contact darkening is a radial-gradient plane.** Real ambient occlusion in the joints and under the raised floor is the largest remaining quality gain. Do not ship the placeholder as the answer.
- **The tongkonan's body walls are vertical.** The real ones lean outward toward the plate. No source gives an angle, so it is left flat rather than guessed — see the note in `toraja/frame.ts`. The rumah gadang *does* lean, at a declared and openly interpolated 8°, which is the same guess made visibly instead of avoided. The two houses disagreeing about this is a question for Phase C, not a bug.
- **The rumah gadang carries four gonjong under both laras.** Houses with anjuang are commonly said to carry more, and the extra ones belong to a roof over the projecting bay — a bay this model does not build. The first attempt stood the extra pair on the middle of the main ridge, which is a shape nobody builds. Four until the bay exists; the laras stays legible in the floor, which is where the sources put it.
- **Minangkabau carving is two motifs, constructed from their description.** Pucuak rabuang and kaluak paku, both named in every published account, both plainly geometric, neither restricted. The proportions are the author's — the same standing as an interpolated dimension. Nothing beyond the two is attempted, because inventing plausible members of a large carving vocabulary would be worse than showing two real ones plainly.
- **The rumah gadang has no derivation and no drawing export.** The tongkonan's `/bangun` shows the arithmetic from three rules to the metres, and exports plan, elevation and section as SVG. Neither is written for the second house: the derivation would have to be worked out rather than generalised, and `lib/draw/` reads a tongkonan's Layout directly — prows, ijuk courses, the knee across the slope — so a second projection is a drawing problem, not a threading one. Both sections are absent for that house rather than wrong, and both components say so at the point they return null.
- **The bale is one building and a Balinese house is a compound.** A walled yard with several bale around a natah, a shrine in the kaja-kangin corner, and a gate you have to turn to get past — so the most distinctive thing about Balinese domestic building is exactly what this models none of. Same standing as the missing rangkiang and the missing anjuang bay: an absence named on screen, not a claim.
- **The bale carries no carving at all**, and on a Balinese building that is a larger omission than it was on a mbaru niang, which genuinely has none. The precedent stands anyway: inventing plausible members of a vocabulary belonging to particular carvers is worse than showing none. It is stated in the caution rather than hidden.
- **The body ratios are the author's anthropometry.** A quarter of an arm span to a hasta and so on. They are tagged `interpolated` against their own source key, `anthropometry`, so the table can say "not from a book about Bali" rather than filing them with numbers nobody thought about.
- **No survey is wired in, for any house.** Everything above follows from that.

**The first hard split: `lib/` generates, the renderer draws.** `lib/` must never import three.js, touch `window`, or read the DOM. The renderer must never generate geometry. If a shape is being computed inside a component, it is in the wrong file.

**The third hard split: the app may not know which house it has.** `lib/tradition/registry.ts` seals each rule type inside its entry. A route, a rail or the renderer takes a `Built` — parts, a scene model, a timeline, verdicts, provenance — and nothing in it names a rank or a laras. The renderer draws `House<Kinds>`, which was already neutral; the registry is what was still stopping the app from using it.

The exception is the rule controls, and it is deliberate. A data-driven field schema would render both houses from one component and would cost the rank multiplier printed on the rank that applies it, the warning when a bay count is unusual, and the gloss saying that refusing a step *is* the statement. Those are the parts of the interface carrying the argument. So each tradition keeps its own controls under `components/rules/` and they share primitives: **the abstraction goes under the widgets, not over them.**

**The second hard split: `lib/core/` may not know what it is building.** It is generic over a `Kinds` bag — a tradition declares its own stages, materials, sources, dimension keys and joinery, and the core is parameterised on them. So `Part.stage` is not one of nine Toraja words at the type level; it is whatever the tradition binding says it is, and a Minang part cannot claim a Toraja stage and type-check.

The direction is one-way and enforced: a tradition imports the core, the core imports nothing from a tradition and mentions none by name in code. When a change to the core would be easier if it could read a real value out of the Toraja pack, that is the abstraction being wrong, not a reason to add the import.

Both splits are what make the geometry testable. Neither is a style preference.

**Concrete aliases at the boundary.** Each tradition re-exports the core types bound to itself (`export type Part = CorePart<TorajaKinds>`), so the generator and the renderer say `Part`, not `Part<TorajaKinds>`, and a `switch` over a material is still exhaustively checked. Generic-over-`Kinds` costs nothing at the point of use, and it is meant to stay that way.

**One `Dim` knows its source key, not its own key.** `Dim<S extends string>` is parameterised on the source table alone. Parameterising it on the full `Kinds` would be circular — `Kinds['dim']` is `keyof typeof DIMS`, so a `Dim` would be defined in terms of the table it is an entry in.

## The address

The URL has two halves and they mean different things.

- **The path is the tradition.** `/[locale]/[tradisi]/[route]`. A tradition selects a rule pack rather than being one of its rules, and putting it in the query would make one string mean two kinds of thing at once. The tradition switch keeps the route and drops the query, because `?pangkat=layuk` means nothing to a rumah gadang and carrying it across would hand the reader an address silently describing a different house from the one they left.
- **The query string is the house**, in that tradition's own parameter names: `?pangkat=…&ruang=…&tanduk=…` for one, `?laras=…&ruang=…&bilik=…` for the other. Every rule always written, defaults included — it is a complete description anyone can cite, and a description that omits its defaults is a diff instead. The mechanism is `lib/core/address.ts`; each tradition declares only which fields exist and what they are called.
- **The fragment is the reader.** Camera, date, time, toggles, explode, stage, section. Written only where it differs from the default, so an untouched page has no fragment at all. Never reaches a server, never reloads. `lib/reader.ts`.

Rules go in the query string, vantage goes in the fragment, and nothing crosses. The two writers each preserve the other half, read live at write time; a test holds that. Vantage writes are debounced 250ms because a dragged slider would otherwise hit the browser's history rate limit.

Playing state is deliberately not in the address. A link that starts animating at someone is a link nobody wants twice.

## Coordinates and units

- Metres throughout. No arbitrary units, no scaling factors in the renderer.
- X runs front (north, negative) to rear (south, positive).
- Y is up. Ground is y = 0.
- Z is transverse. The building is bilaterally symmetric about z = 0.

The north–south axis is baked in because orientation is a rule, not a parameter. Do not add a building rotation.

X-runs-front-to-rear is the shared convention; *what decides which end is the front* is the tradition's own rule, and the Toraja answer — ulunna banua faces north — is Toraja, not general. A second tradition declares its own orientation constraint. It still gets no control.

## The generator contract

`buildHouse(rules)` → `{ house, layout }`. Pure, deterministic, no unseeded randomness, no `Date.now()`. The same call runs in the browser and in the test suite and must produce identical output.

Parts are either:
- **boxes** — centre, size, optional XYZ-order Euler rotation. Boxes stay boxes so joint containment has something exact to test against.
- **meshes** — explicit positions/normals/uvs/indices, in world coordinates.

Every part carries `stage` and `order`. Together they are the build sequence, which the assembly animation walks and the invariants check. `order` is not a z-index.

## Provenance

Every dimension in `rules.ts` is wrapped with its class (`measured` / `canon` / `interpolated`) and a source key. This is not optional metadata — the UI reads it, `/sumber` lists it, and a test prints the interpolated share.

When adding a dimension, tag it honestly. If you invented the number, it is `interpolated` with source `none`. Do not tag a plausible guess as `canon` because a source discusses the feature qualitatively.

**A number is a dimension if changing it changes the size, position or shape of something the reader can see.** Those go in `DIMS` — never inline in a builder, however small or however obviously right. Mesh tessellation counts and UV scales are not dimensions and stay as literals. A test greps the builders for decimals multiplied by the rank scale, because the failure mode is reaching for a number inline when declaring it is slower.

Replacing an interpolated value with a measured one should be a two-line edit: change the value, change the class, point at the survey. Nothing downstream may need to know.

**Provenance is never merged across traditions.** Two houses have two source tables and two interpolated shares, shown separately. An averaged figure over both would be the single most dishonest number this project could print — a house nobody has surveyed hiding behind a house somebody has. `provenanceSplit` takes one tradition's dimensions at a time and that is deliberate.

## Invariants

`pnpm check` type-checks and runs the invariant suite across **at least four rule combinations**, not just the default. The full list is in PRD.md. Rules:

- A failing invariant fails the build. No skipping to unblock a feature.
- `checkAgainstSurvey` reports **skipped** until a real measured drawing exists. Never soften it to green. It is the only check that cannot be satisfied by better code, and that is the point.
- When adding geometry, add the invariant that would catch it being wrong. A render that looks right but violates symmetry or leaves the frame bare under a course is still wrong.

Use exact AABBs for rotated boxes (`|R| · halfExtents`), not a diagonal pad — a coarse bound produces false failures on long leaning members.

## Testing

- Generator and solar engine: Vitest, pure unit tests, no browser.
- Solar engine is validated against known almanac values, not against itself.
- The renderer is not unit-tested. Its correctness gate is the invariant suite plus a human looking at it.
- `test/architecture.test.ts` tests the hard splits directly — the core naming no tradition, and no tradition importing another — because they are properties of the file layout that nothing else would notice breaking. Its naming check strips comments and keeps string literals: the prose has to be able to say "the core may not know a Toraja word" without being the violation it describes, and the thing worth catching is a `stage === 'ijuk'` branch, not a sentence.

## Conventions

- Indonesian is the default locale; English is second. Toraja terms are used as the names of the parts in both.
- UI copy: sentence case, plain verbs, active voice. A control says what happens when it is used.
- Prose is 16px. One-line glosses attached to a control and mono figures are 13px, because they are labels on a thing rather than sentences. Nothing else sits between them.
- Text pairs clear 4.5:1 and interactive targets clear 24px. When a pigment has to carry meaning and cannot clear the floor as a fill, give it an ink variant for its stroke rather than changing the pigment.
- Comments explain *why*, especially where a choice encodes something about the building. `// the eave oversails the posts so the drip line clears the post feet` is worth writing; `// set the eave` is not.
- No `any`. No non-null assertions to silence the checker.
- Commit messages state what changed in the model, not what changed in the code.

## Hard rules

1. **No idle rotation, ever.** Rotation is drag-only. A spinning model reads as a screensaver and tells the reader the object is decorative. Scripted view *transitions* are allowed; a turntable is not.
2. **No building rotation control.** Orientation is a constraint.
3. `lib/` stays pure. No three.js, no DOM, no randomness.
4. No downloaded textures, no photographic assets, no runtime network.
5. Provenance visible on every screen that shows a dimension.
6. `checkAgainstSurvey` stays skipped until a survey exists.
7. No morph between traditions.
8. `prefers-reduced-motion` gets a complete alternative, never a removed feature. The frame-raising sequence is content: de-animate it, do not delete it.

## The second tradition

Phase 2 is real and these decisions are made. Phase A has landed; B and C have not.

- **Phase A — done.** Split `lib/core/` from `lib/tradition/toraja/`, extracting only what is mechanically neutral. No new concepts invented, no user-visible change, tests and provenance figures identical before and after.
- **Phase B — done.** The rumah gadang, built concretely in `lib/tradition/minang/` and allowed to duplicate. Generator, rule pack, source table, invariants and tests; not routed.
- **Phase C — done.** Five extractions the two houses earned, a registry, a scene model, the renderer generic over both, the route, the copy, and the rename.
- **Phase D — done.** A third house: the joglo. Built to settle the questions two houses could not, and it settled them mostly by refusing to fit.

### What the third house settled

- **`sweepSurface` is not the roof primitive, it is the swept-roof primitive.** `minang/roof.ts` asked whether a third house turning the sweep meant the axis belonged in `SweepOptions`. The joglo declined the ballot: a hipped roof has a ridge shorter than its building and four planes falling to a closed eave, and no amount of sweeping makes one. `jawa/hip.ts` is a second primitive standing beside the first, not a generalisation of it. It moves to the core when a second house hips.
- **`ridgeCurve` stays two functions.** `minang/ridge.ts` was waiting for a third vote. It got an abstention: this ridge is a straight segment and has no curve to share.
- **`Layout` is still three `Layout`s**, and the third is the least like the others — rings of pillars, a corbelled stack, a stack of roof rectangles.
- **Not every house is raised.** `SceneModel.underfloorHeight` assumed a habitable void; a joglo has a plinth you cannot get under. The field reports the clearance honestly and the difference between the traditions is an order of magnitude, which is the difference between a storey and a step.
- **Not every house divides vertically.** `SceneModel.zones` is a stack of horizontal bands because that is what two houses needed. This one divides from the centre outward — under the brunjung against under the penanggap — and the bands are the closest honest reading of that, not the thing itself. A fourth house should not be made to pretend otherwise. See the note at the head of `jawa/scene.ts`.
- **~~Not every site has a zero-shadow day.~~ This bullet was wrong and is kept as a correction.** It claimed Yogyakarta at 7.8° south never sees the sun overhead. The tropic runs to 23.44°, so every site in this project is inside it, and `zeroShadowDays` returns 28 February and 13 October 2025 for Yogyakarta. The app has always printed the correct date; the December-solstice fallback has never been taken by any site here. What was true and is worth keeping: the original code fell back to a March date *and kept the label*, which would have printed a day on which nothing happens and called it a zero-shadow day. The fallback is right to exist and right to say plainly that there is no zenith — it is just untested, and the reason given for it was false.
- **A rule can be a flag.** Both earlier packs had only choices and counts; the pendhapa is present or absent, so `rulesCodec` grew a `flag` field rather than the house pretending a boolean was a number.
- **A test written against two examples asserted uniqueness where the truth was variety.** `registry.test.ts` required every tradition to run its ridge on a different axis — true of two, false of three. What matters is that they disagree at all, which is why the field exists.
- **`courseBands` turned out to be about lapping, not about thatch.** Extracted when two thatched roofs agreed; it holds for fired clay on a hip, which is the first evidence it was extracted for the right reason.
- **And what came through untouched:** the whole generic half of the invariant suite, for the third time, which is now reasonable evidence that it is generic.

Three houses, three different ways for a social fact to become a dimension, and no two of them the same shape: the tongkonan has a rank that scales everything, the rumah gadang has a switch that is legible in the floor, and the joglo has a graded series and a tier count that state standing without counting anything. There is still no shared `Rules` interface and there should not be one.

### What the fifth house settled

- **`steppedHip` is a primitive, and the proof was the trivial case.** It moves to the core, as `jawa/hip.ts` said it would. What settled it was not a second elaborate hipped roof but a roof with one band, which the code handled without a line changed.
- **`sweepSurface`'s axis question is still open, and now permanently.** Two houses sweep, and the third and fifth do not sweep at all. There is no third vote coming from this direction; if the axis ever belongs in `SweepOptions` it will be because a *sweeping* house wants it.
- **A rule can be a measurement of a person.** `rulesCodec` needed nothing — an integer field is an integer field — which is the right outcome: the mechanism's job is fields, and what a field means is the tradition's business. But it is the first control in the project that wants a slider rather than radios, because an arm span is not enumerable and four preset body sizes would have said something false.
- **A dimension can have two provenances, and they must not be merged.** See above. This is the first pack where one class per number was not enough.
- **A check can pass by finding a disagreement.** `checkPengurip` is the only invariant here whose success condition is that something does *not* line up. It also gives the project its first counterexample that produces a perfectly constructible building — every earlier one ends with something that cannot be built, and this one ends with something that merely must not be.
- **`underfloorHeight` has now carried four meanings** — a storey, a thatched metre and a quarter, a plinth, and something you sit on the edge of. It still reports a clearance and lets the number say which, which remains the right answer and is now well tested.
- **`zones` fits for a second time and for a different reason.** The mbaru niang's five floors are a stack because the building is stacked; the bale's three bands are the tri angga, a division the tradition states about the building. One field, one physical arrangement and one named cultural claim, both honest.
- **Five houses, five different ways for a social fact to become a dimension, and the fifth is the odd one out**: a rank that scales, a switch legible in the floor, a graded series with a tier count, a household tally — and then a house where the social fact is not *which number* but *whose body the numbers are counted in*. There is still no shared `Rules` interface and there should not be one.
- **And a correction the fifth house's site turned up:** the solar preset comment claimed Yogyakarta at 7.8° south never sees the sun overhead. It does — the tropic runs to 23.44° and every site here is inside it. See the note under the third house.

### What Phase C settled

- **What was extracted, and only because two houses agreed:** lapped courses, the box and mesh builders, the rules codec, the sensitivity probe, the counterexample search. Each had been written twice and each pair was the same arithmetic over different constants.
- **What was not, and stays not:** `Layout`. There is still no shared one and now for a second reason — the second carries anjuang, gonjong tips, lanjar and bilik, and runs its ridge on the other axis. What a probe should measure and which check is worth breaking are judgements about a particular building; the mechanisms moved and the judgements did not.
- **The renderer needed a narrower question than `Layout` answers.** Not "what are this house's dimensions" but "where does the water land, how does it divide vertically, how far does the roof reach, which way is it long". That question two houses could answer, and `SceneModel` is the answer.
- **A schema for controls was declined**, with reasons, above.
- **Extracting found two disagreements and one bug.** The two address codecs disagreed about whether `?tanduk=` means six horns or none — it means a truncated address, so it falls back. The counterexample search found that an anjuang step shallower than the boards forming it produced a bearer of negative height. And `components/controls/` collided with `Controls.tsx` on a case-insensitive filesystem, which is now a test that reads from git rather than the disk, because the machine most likely to introduce that collision is the one that cannot hold it.

### What the second house taught

Written down while it is fresh, because this is the entire reason for building it.

**The core came through almost unchanged**, which is the main result. Joints, build order, joint stages, mesh integrity, part provenance and the survey check all ran against the rumah gadang as written. Only `checkSymmetry` needed touching, and both changes it needed were real:

- The plane needed a *label*. Both houses mirror about z = 0, but this one's ridge runs *along* that plane rather than across it — so "the ridge plane" was Toraja wording that happened to be true, not a general fact. The X-runs-front-to-rear convention held; the relationship between the ridge and the mirror did not.
- The claim needed a *scope*. The rumah gadang is symmetric in its frame and deliberately asymmetric in its bilik, because the bilik are a tally that fills from one end. A check over everything would have had to be false or be softened; scoped to the frame, and paired with `checkBilikTally` stating the sequence positively, it says two true things. The verdict prints how many parts were left out, so a narrowed claim can never read as a whole-building one.

**What broke that would have broken a premature abstraction:**

- **There is no rank scale.** Every Toraja dimension passes through one multiplier set by rank, because there the social parameter governs *size*. Here the social parameter governs *shape* — whether the floor steps — and size comes from the plan counts. A shared `scale` field would have been the first casualty.
- **`Layout` is still not shareable**, and now for a second reason: this one carries anjuang, gonjong tips, lanjar and bilik, and its ridge runs on the other axis.
- **A declared dimension in one house is derived in the other.** `roofKneeDrop` is a guess for the tongkonan because nothing pins its break to a height; here the rafters bear on the wall plate, so the knee is arithmetic. The first draft declared it anyway and put the whole roof 100 mm above the plate it sits on — the joint invariant caught it. Fewer invented numbers, and a roof that touches its own frame.
- **Orientation is a constraint in both and not the same kind of constraint.** Toraja is absolute — the front faces north. Minang is relational — the front faces the halaman with the rangkiang across it. Neither gets a control; a shared `orientation: degrees` would have been wrong for one of them.

**What is now demonstrably shared and is Phase C's extraction list:** the `ijukBands` course algorithm (identical logic, different constants), the `box`/`meshPart` builders, the `sAtX`/`sAtZ` ridge parameterisation, the plate–rafter–purlin pattern, and the shape of `address.ts`. `sweepSurface` is shared already, reached by a quarter turn (`swapXZ`) rather than an axis flag — two houses is not yet a pattern, so if a third also turns it, the axis belongs in `SweepOptions`.

**One thing the build order taught about the building itself:** over the body the roof is carried from below — the lower rafter lands on the plate, the upper meets it at the knee. Over the two gable overhangs there is no plate, so the upper rafter hangs off the ridge and the lower hangs off that. The invariant refused the overhang until that was written down, which is a check earning its keep: a cantilever built from the bottom up is a cantilever with nothing holding it.

Why gadang and not Batak Toba: Toba is nearly isomorphic to the tongkonan — single sagging ridge, boat roof, raised on posts — so it would go fast and teach nothing, and it would produce an abstraction that is tongkonan-shaped while appearing validated. Gadang refuses to fit, which is the point. The Koto Piliang / Bodi Caniago split (raised end platforms vs a flat floor) is a socially-loaded geometric switch of the same kind as rank, and the multi-gonjong roof genuinely breaks `roofStations`, which sweeps one section along one ridge.

Decisions taken, so they are not re-litigated:

- **Tradition is a path segment, not a query parameter:** `/[locale]/[tradition]/bangun?…`. The query string stays "the house" and each tradition declares its own param names. A tradition selects a rule pack rather than being a rule, and putting it in the query would make that string mean two kinds of thing at once.
- **The project gets renamed to Pasak** — the peg that pins a mortise and tenon. Standard Indonesian, belongs to no one tradition, and it names the join rather than the house. The rename executes in Phase B, when there are two houses; calling a one-tongkonan app Pasak before then is a promise the app does not keep.
- **Hard rule 7 gets stronger, not weaker.** A tradition switcher is a cut: unmount, remount, no crossfade, no shared camera easing. The morph is forbidden because it asserts a continuity that does not exist, and a switcher is where that temptation will actually arrive.
- **The survey is still the higher-value work.** A second tradition does not move the interpolated bar; it adds a second bar that starts worse. If a measured drawing of a named tongkonan becomes obtainable, it pre-empts Phase B.

## Things that will be tempting and are wrong

- **Adding a roof-shape slider.** The roof is downstream of the rules. If the shape needs adjusting, adjust the rule pack and say why in the provenance note.
- **Trusting a green build for a route nobody opened.** Three houses were added, `pnpm check` passed and `pnpm build` emitted every page — and `/dayak/bangun` threw on load. The material set is built from a `Record<string, Material>`, so a missing key is invisible to the type checker; the share cards come from `lib/core/silhouette.ts`, which is pure, so they never touch the material layer at all. **A build that succeeds is not a page that renders**, and the og card is not the human looking at it. `components/materials.keys.ts` and `test/materials.test.ts` now close the gap: the keys are pure data, and the tests ask what every tradition's *parts* actually request against what it is entitled to. Writing them found a second fault immediately — the uma asked for `alang`, which nothing declared, and in the static export that does not throw, it silently falls back, so the whole thatched tower would have been drawn in timber.
- **Splitting a shared thing by name and thinking it is split.** The material sets were split per tradition and the carving *construction* behind `ukiran` stayed shared, so the rumah gadang wore the Toraja sun disc across its front for a whole phase. `SHARED_MATERIALS` and `OWN_MATERIALS` in `components/materials.ts` now say which is which, and carving is listed as each tradition's own even though both houses have one.
- **Making the render prettier with bloom, vignette, DOF, or a HDRI.** See DESIGN.md. The register is a physical model under real light, and effects on top of interpolated numbers are a lie told fluently.
- **Abstracting further before the second house exists.** Phase A extracted only what is mechanically neutral — parts, joints, build order, mesh integrity, provenance, the geometry primitives — and it did so without inventing a single shared concept. Everything past that line waits. `Layout` is two thirds Toraja roof and stays tradition-side; there is no shared `Rules` interface, because a rank and a Minang lineage-system switch have nothing in common but being said out loud; the control schema, the tradition registry and the renderer adapter are all Phase C. An abstraction designed from one example comes out tongkonan-shaped and, worse, looks validated.
- **Hardcoding a dimension in the renderer** because it is faster than threading it through the layout. It breaks the split and it silently escapes the provenance layer.
- **Reaching for a mesh library** for CSG, lofting, or subdivision. Generation is the subject.

## Ambient occlusion

Real AO in the joints and under the raised floor is the single largest quality gain available and worth doing properly once the register is settled. A radial-gradient contact plane under the body is an acceptable placeholder; do not ship it as the final answer. Do not pull in a post-processing stack before M2 is judged.
